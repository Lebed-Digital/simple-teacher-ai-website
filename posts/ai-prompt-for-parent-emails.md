I had a parent email sitting in drafts for two days last spring. I knew what I wanted to say. I also knew that if I typed it the way it was sitting in my head, it would come out too blunt. So I left it there, which did not help anyone.

What I do now is type the blunt version on purpose. Then I ask AI to make it sound like a person a parent would want to hear from. I still read every word. I still change lines. But I am not staring at a blank email for twenty minutes anymore.

## What I actually type

I do not start with "write a parent email." That is how you get something that sounds like a newsletter and says nothing.

I write the notes the way I would say them in the hallway. Messy is fine. Too honest is fine. That is the point. Last spring one of them looked like: "didn't turn in homework three times this week, still doing the work in class, don't want this to sound like I am mad at the parent." Ugly. Also everything the email actually needed.

Then I paste this:

```
Turn my rough notes below into a clear, friendly parent email. Keep it warm and professional, under 150 words, and easy to read on a phone. Do not add anything I did not say. Do not promise anything I cannot guarantee. Do not sound defensive.

My notes:
[PASTE YOUR NOTES HERE]
```

That "do not add anything" line matters. Left alone, it will invent a compliment I did not give, or a next step I did not plan. I would rather have a short email that is true than a longer one I have to unwrite.

I have used this in ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini. Same idea in all three.

## If a parent already wrote me

That is a different job, because now I am answering someone.

I paste a version of their email with the names pulled out, or I summarize what they asked in one or two sentences. Then I ask for three versions: warm, brief, and more detailed. I pick one and edit it. I do not send all three. I do not send the first one without reading it.

There is a copy of that prompt on the [home page](/#prompts) if you want it already filled in.

## What I take out first

I still take names out. "The student" is enough. The AI does not need a real name to help me reword a paragraph, and [a kid's name does not belong in an AI tool](/blog/ai-privacy-mistake-student-names). Same for a diagnosis, a family situation, or anything from an IEP.

If the email is about something sensitive, I often write that one myself. Same rule I use for [report card comments](/blog/ai-prompt-for-report-card-comments). Some messages need me sitting with them longer than a draft is worth.

## What I still change by hand

Tone. If a line sounds like a form letter, I rewrite it in my own words.

Facts. If it added a conference time I did not offer, that line is gone.

Anything that promises a result. "He will be caught up by Friday" is not something I let a draft say unless I actually said that.

This is the same pattern as [the rest of how I use AI](/blog/how-i-actually-use-ai-as-a-teacher). I do the noticing. It does the wording. I check it before I hit send.

## Try it on the email you have been putting off

Open the one sitting in drafts. Type the blunt version, paste the prompt, and see if the draft is closer than the blank screen was.

If the first answer is too stiff, tell it. "Too formal." "Shorter." "Do not add a compliment I did not give." I do that more than I start over, which is the same habit I use when [AI keeps handing me generic teacher stuff](/blog/why-ai-keeps-giving-generic-teacher-stuff).
