A teacher in my building asked me last month which AI she should start with. ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini. She said she did not want to pick the wrong one and have to learn it twice.

I did not have a clean answer for her, because I do not think there is a wrong one for the work most of us are trying to do. I bounce between all three. I have used each of them for a parent email, a set of directions, and a quick exit ticket, and I have gotten something I could actually use from all three.

What I told her was to pick one and use it for a week. You can always switch later. I have wasted a planning period comparing tabs. It did not make the email better.

## What I actually use them for

The jobs are small. [Parent emails](/blog/ai-prompt-for-parent-emails). [Directions](/blog/ai-prompt-rewrite-student-directions). Exit tickets. [Report card comments](/blog/ai-prompt-for-report-card-comments) when I already have notes. Asking where a [lesson might fall apart](/blog/ai-prompt-find-where-students-will-struggle) before I teach it.

None of that needs a special tool. It needs a clear prompt and me reading the draft.

I opened ChatGPT first, so that is still the one I reach for out of habit. I use Claude a lot now. Gemini is the one I grab when I am already in my Google account. That is not a ranking. That is just how my tabs are arranged on a Sunday night.

## Privacy first

For anything involving student information, privacy matters more than which name is on the page. Some tools, like Claude for Teachers, add K-12 privacy protections and do not use what you type to train the model. That is a real difference from a regular consumer account.

It still does not mean you should paste a kid's name in. I [take names out first](/blog/ai-privacy-mistake-student-names) no matter which tab I am in. District policy still applies either way. I would rather use a slightly less convenient tool I am allowed to use than a nicer one I am not.

If your district has named a tool, start there. That decision is already made.

## Free versus paid is a different question

Teachers also ask me if they should pay. I wrote that up separately, because [free versus paid](/blog/free-vs-paid-ai-tools-for-teachers) is about usage limits, not about ChatGPT being "better" than Claude.

For a short email or three exit ticket questions, the free versions have been enough for me. I hit a wall when I try to do a whole class of something in one sitting. If you do not work that way, you may never need to pay.

You do not need to pay to find out if this is useful. Start free.

## What I would not do

I would not open all three and try to learn them at once. That is how you spend your planning period comparing tools instead of finishing the email.

I would not pick a tool because a list on the internet said one was the best this month. Those lists go stale, and they are usually ranking features I do not use.

I also would not use any of them for something I cannot check. If I cannot tell whether the draft is right, I have no business sending it, which is the same line I draw in [how I actually use AI](/blog/how-i-actually-use-ai-as-a-teacher).

## If you are starting from zero

Open whichever one you can get to fastest. Paste one real task you already have to do this week. Use a short starter so it knows your classroom, the same four blanks I use when [the first answer comes back generic](/blog/why-ai-keeps-giving-generic-teacher-stuff).

If you want a prompt already filled in, I keep a few on the [home page](/#prompts). The tool you paste them into matters less than doing that once.
