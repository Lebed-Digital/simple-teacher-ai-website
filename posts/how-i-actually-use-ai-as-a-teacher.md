Last Sunday I had to prepare my math lesson about telling time, write three parent emails I had been putting off, and look over a set of exit tickets due Monday morning. I opened ChatGPT, mostly out of laziness, and by the time I closed my laptop I had done all three in under forty minutes. That is the honest version of how AI fits into my teaching life. It gets me through the boring parts faster so I have more time for the parts that actually need me.

I want to be specific here. Most of what I read about AI in education is either people acting like it changes everything or people warning you away from it. Neither one tells you what to do on a Tuesday afternoon when you have twenty minutes before your next class.

## Where it actually helps

Parent emails are the biggest one. I type out my notes the way I would say them out loud, which is usually a little too blunt to send, and ask AI to make it sound calm and professional. I read every word before it goes out. But getting from a blank screen to a first draft used to eat my whole planning period, and now it takes about a minute.

Rewriting word problems is the second one. I teach the same math skill to kids who are all over the place with numbers, and writing three versions of every problem set by hand was never actually going to happen. Now I write it once at grade level and ask for a version with smaller numbers or fewer steps. I still fix it up, because AI does not know my kids, but it beats starting from nothing.

Exit tickets are the third. Sometimes I finish teaching something and realize I have nothing ready to check if it stuck. I can describe the lesson in two sentences and get three decent questions back. I change them to match what I actually taught, but the hard part is done.

## Where it does not help

Grading anything that takes judgment. If a kid shows their work on a math problem and gets the wrong answer, the useful part is figuring out where their thinking went sideways. That is the part I care most about, and every time I have tried handing it off I get feedback that sounds fine and says nothing.

I also do not use it for IEP goals or anything tied to a specific kid's documented needs. Those need real thought about a real kid, and getting one wrong is not worth the ten minutes I would save.

And I do not use it to plan a whole unit. I tried. What comes back is a generic version of what I would have written anyway, minus everything that makes it fit my classroom.

## The pattern I have landed on

The stuff that works is the stuff I can check fast, like whether an email sounds calm or an exit ticket matches what I taught or a problem set is actually easier. The stuff that does not work is anything where the right answer depends on knowing a specific kid. That has held up for months now, and it is what I ask myself before I open any AI tool: can I check this in ten seconds, or am I asking it to know something only I know?

If you are starting from zero, pick the one task you dread every week and try it there. For me it was parent emails.
