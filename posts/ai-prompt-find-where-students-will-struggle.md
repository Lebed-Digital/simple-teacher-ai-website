I read the lesson the night before, and it looked fine. Clear objective, clear steps, nothing weird. Then I taught it, and about five minutes in, half the class stalled on one word I did not even think twice about. That is the part nobody warns you about with lesson plans. They read clean. They do not teach clean.

I started doing one thing before I teach anything now, and it has saved me more than once. I hand the lesson to AI first and ask where it is going to fall apart.

## The prompt I actually use

I paste in the learning goal, the grade level, and a short outline of what I am teaching. Then I ask this:

```
I am about to teach this lesson to a [grade level] class.

The learning goal is: [your learning goal]

Here is a short outline of the lesson: [your outline]

Look for:
- vocabulary that might trip students up
- likely misconceptions
- steps in the directions that could confuse them
- places where another example would help
- background knowledge they might be missing

Do not rewrite the lesson. Give me a short list I can glance at before I teach.
```

That last line matters. Left alone, AI will happily rewrite your whole lesson for you, and then you are reading a new lesson instead of teaching the one you planned. I do not want that. I want a heads-up, not a rewrite.

## What actually comes back

Usually it is stuff I half-knew and had not thought about that day. A vocabulary word that sounds simple to an adult and means nothing to an eight-year-old. A direction with three steps buried in one sentence. Or it flags a misconception that shows up almost every year, like students mixing up the number of groups with the number in each group during multiplication. I probably would have caught it anyway, but now I catch it before the lesson instead of during it.

It is not always right. Sometimes it flags something my kids would never struggle with, and I just ignore that line. I am not looking for a perfect list. I am looking for the one thing I would have missed.

## What I do not paste in

I do not copy an entire curriculum lesson into an AI tool. A lot of curriculum publishers do not allow that, and I do not need the whole document anyway. A short outline in my own words works just as well and keeps me on the right side of whatever my district's materials policy says.

I also never put a student's name or any real details about a specific kid into this prompt, and you should not either. This one does not need it. "Where will students struggle with this lesson" does not require knowing who your students are, and [that is generally the safest way to think about anything you paste into AI](/blog/ai-privacy-mistake-student-names).

## This is not lesson planning

I want to be clear about what this is not. I am not asking AI to write my lesson or plan my unit. I already have the lesson. I am asking it to read over my shoulder and point at the parts likely to cause trouble, the way a second teacher would if you handed them your plan and said "does anything jump out." That narrow job is why it works so well. I am not asking AI to plan the lesson. I am asking it to help me spot problems before I teach it, which is the same habit that turned into [one of the most useful things I do most nights](/blog/is-it-okay-for-teachers-to-use-ai-lesson-plans).

## Try it on tomorrow's lesson

Pull up whatever you are teaching next, grab the learning goal and a two or three sentence outline, and run it through the prompt above. You are not looking for a perfect answer. You are looking for the one word, the one step, the one misconception that would have caught you off guard five minutes into class.

That is the whole idea behind [how I use AI as a teacher](/blog/how-i-actually-use-ai-as-a-teacher) generally: it does not need to know my kids to be useful. It just needs to catch the thing I read past.
