The fire department was coming in for a fire prevention assembly. Great for the kids. Not so great for my plans. I had a 120-minute CKLA lesson on main idea and supporting details for my third graders, and the assembly left me 60 minutes to teach it. On top of that, several kids had been absent the day before and needed to make up an activity they missed.

The curriculum wasn't going to get shorter on its own. I didn't need ChatGPT to write a new lesson or decide what my students should learn. I needed help taking the lesson I already had and making it fit the hour I actually had.

So yes, free ChatGPT can give you a lesson plan you can actually teach. You don't need a paid tool or a special lesson plan generator. You do have to give it the right information, and then treat what comes back as a draft. Here's how I do it, including the part where its first plan didn't work for my room.

## Why "write a lesson plan on fractions" doesn't work

You can open ChatGPT, type "write a third-grade lesson plan on fractions," and get something that looks perfectly reasonable: an objective, a warm-up, a few activities, an assessment. But it doesn't know how long your math block is, what your kids learned yesterday, or what your curriculum already has planned. It definitely doesn't know the fire department just took half your morning.

That's why so many AI lesson plans look impressive and still don't fit anybody's classroom. I wrote more about this in [why AI keeps giving you generic teacher stuff](/blog/why-ai-keeps-giving-generic-teacher-stuff). The fix usually isn't a better AI. It's giving the one you have better information.

## What to have ready before you open ChatGPT

Gather five things first:

1. **Grade and subject.** What you're teaching, and to whom.
2. **The objective.** What students actually need to learn, from your standard or curriculum.
3. **The lesson content.** A short summary of what the curriculum has you do.
4. **Your real minutes.** Not the minutes on the schedule. The ones you actually have.
5. **One thing about your class.** Kids who missed yesterday, a group that needs more guided practice, an assembly that ate half your block.

You can describe all of that without naming anyone. Keep student names and personal details out of the prompt. That's the [AI privacy mistake teachers keep making](/blog/ai-privacy-mistake-student-names), and it's an easy one to avoid. Also check your district's rules before pasting curriculum materials into any AI tool.

## The ChatGPT lesson plan prompt I use

Copy this into free ChatGPT and fill in the brackets:

```
I'm teaching [grade] [subject] from my curriculum.
Objective: [objective]
Lesson summary: [what the curriculum has me do]
Students already know: [prior learning]
Time I actually have: [number] minutes
Something about my class today: [general situation, no names]

Help me adapt this existing lesson. Include:
1. A kid-friendly objective
2. A timed plan that adds up to exactly my minutes
3. The part students are most likely to get stuck on, and how to teach it
4. A quick check for understanding
5. What to cut first if I run short on time

Don't add materials I didn't mention. Keep it short and realistic.
```

Notice what it's asking for. I'm not asking ChatGPT to replace my curriculum or invent a lesson. I'm asking it to reshape the lesson I'm already responsible for so it fits my day.

## A real example: when 120 minutes became 60

Here's that prompt filled in for the assembly day. I didn't save my exact wording from that morning, so this is written out for this post using the same situation:

```
I'm teaching third-grade ELA from the CKLA curriculum. The lesson is on identifying the main idea and supporting details. It's designed for 120 minutes, but a school assembly cut my time to 60. Several students were absent yesterday and need to make up an activity they missed, and I'll need time to help them.

Help me adapt the existing lesson to fit 60 minutes without changing its objective. Include a timed plan, real student practice, a quick check for understanding, and what to shorten. Don't add new materials or activities that aren't part of the lesson.
```

I didn't save ChatGPT's response either, so I won't pretend to quote it. But I remember the shape of it. It was a reasonable plan, and the minutes added up, but most of it had me at the front of the room leading the lesson.

That was the problem. I couldn't lead for most of the hour. I had kids who needed me to walk them through an activity they had missed, and I can't do that while I'm running a whole-class lesson.

### What I changed

So I flipped it. I turned the teacher-led parts into partner work. Kids worked together to find the main idea and pick out the details that support it, and that freed me to sit with the students who had been absent.

It worked. We finished the lesson, and the kids who had been absent finished their makeup work. In this lesson, I think the kids got more out of talking through the material together than they would have from listening to me, and maybe tuning me out.

That's the real point of this whole post. ChatGPT helped me organize the lesson, but it didn't know my room. I did. If you know you'll need to pull a small group, put that in the prompt. If you forget, the second follow-up below fixes it in one step.

## Follow-up prompts that make it better

You don't have to take the first answer. Tell it what isn't working. A few to try:

```
Make this fit 40 minutes without changing the objective.
Turn the teacher-led parts into partner work so I can work with a small group.
Write a 3-question exit ticket for this lesson, with answers.
What can I shorten and still keep the essential learning?
```

The second one is the assembly-day fix in one line. Partner work isn't always the answer, though. It depends on the skill and on what your kids can do without you. ## What I don't let ChatGPT do

It doesn't decide what my students need to learn. That comes from my curriculum, the standards, and my own judgment. I also check anything it writes that kids will see, like an explanation, an example, or an answer key, because it gets things wrong sometimes.

I wouldn't put an unchecked AI plan in front of an observer or use one for a lesson built around a student's IEP. Those plans need to come from me. If you're wondering whether any of this is okay, I wrote about [whether teachers should use AI to write lesson plans](/blog/is-it-okay-for-teachers-to-use-ai-lesson-plans). For me, it's an assistant, not the teacher.

## Do you need a paid AI lesson planning tool?

No. Everything here works in free ChatGPT. If you teach K-12 in the U.S., you can also get [ChatGPT for Teachers for free](/blog/chatgpt-for-teachers-free). Start with a lesson you already know well, so you can tell when ChatGPT gives you something useful and when it misses.

## Try it with tomorrow's lesson

Next time an assembly, a schedule change, or a pile of makeup work throws off your plan, give ChatGPT your real objective and the minutes you actually have. Then change whatever doesn't fit your room. It can help you build the plan, but you're still the one teaching it.
